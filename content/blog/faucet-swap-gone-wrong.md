A homeowner named Dave (not his real name, but a story we hear versions of often) decided to replace his kitchen faucet on a Saturday afternoon. He'd watched a video the night before, bought a nice new faucet, and figured he'd have it done before lunch. Two hours later, he was standing ankle-deep in water inside his cabinet, on the phone with an emergency plumber, wondering how a job that looked so simple online had gone so wrong.

This kind of story is more common than most people realize, and it's worth understanding exactly where it goes sideways, because the risks aren't obvious until you're already in the middle of them.

## Where the trouble usually starts

Faucet installation itself, once everything is disconnected, is genuinely not that hard. The problem almost never comes from putting the new faucet in. It comes from getting the old one out.

**The shutoff valves.** Under most kitchen and bathroom sinks, there are two small valves that control water to the faucet. In an older home, or a home where those valves haven't been touched in years, they can seize up, corrode internally, or crack the moment you try to turn them. Dave's valve looked fine from the outside. The moment he turned it, the aging brass fitting cracked at the base, and water started spraying before he even had his wrench on the faucet itself.

**The supply line connections.** Supply lines connect the shutoff valve to the faucet, and the fittings on both ends are often decades old, coated in mineral buildup, and reluctant to let go. Forcing a stuck fitting with a wrench, which feels like the obvious next move, is exactly how you strip the threads or snap a corroded nut clean off.

**Mismatched fittings.** New faucets don't always use the same size or thread pattern as the one being replaced, especially if the old faucet is decades old or came from a manufacturer that's since changed designs. Homeowners sometimes assume "a fitting is a fitting," force a connection that isn't quite right, and end up with a leak that only shows up under water pressure, not during a dry test.

## Why this catches people off guard

The videos and tutorials that make faucet swaps look easy are almost always filmed with a cooperative, well-maintained setup: valves that turn smoothly, fittings that separate cleanly, parts that match. Real homes, especially ones more than a decade or two old, don't always cooperate. And the failure points, a cracked valve, a stripped fitting, tend to happen suddenly and without warning, not gradually.

## What to check before you start

- Test your shutoff valves gently before committing to the project. If they feel stiff or don't want to turn, that's a red flag worth addressing first.
- Have a bucket, towels, and your home's main shutoff location ready, just in case.
- Know that once you disturb an old valve or fitting, there's a real chance it won't seal the same way again even if nothing looks broken.
- If your home's shutoff valves haven't been replaced in over 15 to 20 years, budget for the possibility that they'll need attention regardless of how the faucet swap goes.

## The real lesson

Dave's story didn't end in disaster because he made a mistake with the faucet. It ended in a flooded cabinet because the aging valve behind the faucet failed the moment it was touched, something no amount of careful faucet installation would have prevented. That's the part of these projects that's genuinely hard to predict from a video or a guide.
