That unmistakable rotten-egg or sewage smell drifting through your house is unpleasant enough to make anyone assume the worst. Before you picture a major sewer line collapse, though, it's worth knowing that the most common causes of indoor sewer odor are actually pretty simple and often fixable in a few minutes. Here's where to check first.

## Start with dried-out P-traps

Every drain in your home, from sinks to floor drains, has a curved section of pipe underneath called a **P-trap**. Its whole job is to hold a small amount of water that blocks sewer gas from rising up through the drain and into your house. If a drain hasn't been used in a while, a guest bathroom, a basement floor drain, a laundry sink, that water can slowly evaporate, leaving nothing to block the smell.

The fix is almost embarrassingly simple: run water in that drain for 20 to 30 seconds to refill the trap. If the smell disappears, that's your answer. It's worth doing this for every rarely-used drain in the house, since it's often more than one.

## Check for a cracked or failed wax ring at the toilet

The base of a toilet sits on a wax ring that creates an airtight seal between the toilet and the drain pipe below it. If that seal has failed, whether from age, a toilet that's been rocking, or a poor installation, sewer gas can leak out around the base of the toilet instead of staying contained in the pipe.

Signs this might be your issue:

- The smell is strongest near a specific toilet, not throughout the house
- The toilet feels slightly loose or rocks when you sit on it
- You notice water or staining on the floor around the base

A failed wax ring means the toilet needs to be pulled and the ring replaced, which involves resetting the toilet properly to get a good seal.

## Consider a vent stack issue

Your home's plumbing system relies on vent pipes that run up through the roof to let sewer gas escape outside and let air into the drain system so water flows smoothly. If a vent stack becomes blocked, by a bird's nest, debris, or even ice in colder months, gas that should be venting outside can get pushed back into the house instead, sometimes accompanied by gurgling drains.

This is trickier to diagnose from inside the house and often requires checking the roof vent directly, which isn't something most homeowners are equipped or eager to do themselves.

## When it's likely something bigger

If you've refilled every P-trap in the house, the smell isn't isolated to one toilet, and it persists or gets worse, the cause may be further down the line: a cracked sewer pipe, a venting problem beyond a simple blockage, or in rare cases a failing seal somewhere in the main drain system. Persistent sewer odor is also worth taking seriously for safety reasons, since sewer gas contains methane and other compounds that aren't something you want lingering indoors.

## Working through it methodically

1. Run water in every rarely-used drain to refill P-traps
2. Check toilets for rocking, looseness, or staining at the base
3. Note whether the smell is constant or comes and goes, and whether it's tied to a specific room
4. If none of the above resolves it, treat it as a sign something deeper needs a look
