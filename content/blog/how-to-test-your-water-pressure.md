Most homeowners have a vague sense of whether their water pressure feels "fine" or "weak," but few have actually measured it. That's a shame, because testing it is genuinely simple and takes about five minutes. And pressure that's too high can be just as damaging to your plumbing as pressure that's too low, even if it feels great in the shower.

## Why This Is Worth Doing

Water pressure that's too low is annoying, weak showers, slow-filling washing machines, sluggish outdoor hoses. But pressure that's too high is arguably worse for your home long-term, because it stresses fittings, connections, and appliance valves every single time you turn on a faucet. Over months and years, excess pressure contributes to failed supply lines, leaking fittings, and shortened water heater life. Testing tells you which situation you're actually in.

## What You'll Need

- A **water pressure gauge** that threads onto a standard hose bib (widely available and inexpensive)
- An outdoor spigot or, if you don't have one, a laundry room sink with a threaded hose connection
- A few minutes with no other water running in the house

## Step-by-Step Testing

1. **Make sure nothing else is using water.** Turn off dishwashers, washing machines, sprinklers, and ask anyone else in the house to hold off on showers or faucets for the few minutes this takes.
2. **Screw the gauge onto an outdoor hose bib**, hand-tight, the same way you'd attach a garden hose.
3. **Turn the spigot fully on.**
4. **Read the dial.** Most gauges show a needle pointing to the current static pressure in pounds per square inch (psi).
5. **Note the number**, then turn the spigot off and remove the gauge.

## What the Number Means

The generally recommended healthy range for residential water pressure is **40 to 60 psi**. Here's how to interpret your reading:

- **Below 40 psi**: you're likely to notice weak flow at multiple fixtures, especially showers and upper-floor faucets
- **40 to 60 psi**: normal, healthy range
- **60 to 80 psi**: on the high side, may already be stressing fittings and appliance connections
- **Above 80 psi**: considered excessive, and a real risk to pipes, water heater components, and appliance supply lines over time

## If Your Pressure Is Too High

Homes with pressure consistently above 80 psi typically have either no pressure-reducing valve (PRV) installed, or one that's failing and no longer regulating properly. A PRV is usually located near where the main water line enters your home. Adjusting or replacing one requires understanding the specific pressure your municipal supply is sending in, and getting it wrong in either direction causes its own problems, so this is a case where professional adjustment pays off.

## If Your Pressure Is Too Low

Low readings point back to causes like a partially closed valve, a failing PRV set too conservatively, or, in some cases, a municipal supply issue rather than anything specific to your fixtures.

## Testing Regularly Pays Off

Consider testing once a year, or any time you notice a change in how your fixtures feel. It's a cheap habit that catches problems before they show up as a burst supply line or a prematurely failed water heater.
