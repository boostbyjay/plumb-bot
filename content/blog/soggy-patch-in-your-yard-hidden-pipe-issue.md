One patch of your lawn stays soft and soggy no matter the weather, staying greener and squishier than the grass around it. Maybe you've noticed it for a while and assumed it was just a low spot where water naturally collects. Sometimes that's exactly what it is. Other times, there's a pipe underneath that's been leaking for longer than you'd like to think about.

## Innocent explanations worth ruling out first

Not every soggy patch means trouble. Before assuming the worst, consider these more common and harmless causes:

- **Poor grading.** If the soggy area is in a natural low point of your yard, or downhill from a slope, it may simply be where rainwater and runoff naturally collect.
- **A downspout draining nearby.** Check whether a gutter downspout empties near that spot, especially after a recent rain.
- **Overwatering from a sprinkler head.** A head that's misaligned or running too long can consistently overwater one section of lawn.
- **A shallow water table or heavy clay soil.** Some soil types simply drain more slowly than others, creating persistently damp spots in certain seasons.

## Signs that point toward an actual leak

If you've ruled out the obvious explanations above, or the patch is unusually persistent, these signs suggest a buried pipe might be the real cause:

- **The area stays wet even during dry stretches with no rain and no irrigation running.** This is one of the clearest signals, since normal drainage issues dry out eventually.
- **An unusually green or lush patch compared to the rest of the lawn.** A steady, gentle supply of water (and sometimes nutrients from a sewer line) makes grass grow noticeably better right above a leak.
- **A sound of running water when you put your ear near the ground**, particularly if everything inside the house is off.
- **Your water bill has climbed without a clear explanation.**
- **The patch is along the path between your water meter, your house, or your septic system**, rather than in a spot that would naturally collect surface runoff.

## The simple test that can help narrow it down

If you suspect a water line leak specifically, there's a test you can do without any tools: locate your home's water meter, make sure every faucet, appliance, and irrigation zone inside and outside the house is off, then check the meter's leak indicator, usually a small triangular or star-shaped dial. If it's spinning with everything off, water is flowing somewhere it shouldn't be, and a soggy patch along the supply line's likely path becomes a strong suspect.

This test only tells you about the water supply line, though. It won't reveal a leaking sewer line or a failing irrigation line, since those aren't part of your metered water usage in the same way.

## Why this isn't a wait-and-see situation

An underground leak, whether it's your main water line, a sewer line, or an irrigation line, doesn't fix itself, and the soil erosion it can cause over time sometimes leads to settling problems with nearby walkways, patios, or even your foundation if the leak is close enough to the house. What looks like a cosmetic lawn issue today can become a much bigger excavation and repair project the longer it continues.

If your meter test came back positive, or the soggy patch keeps checking boxes on the leak list above, it's worth having it looked at rather than reseeding the same spot every season and wondering why it never holds.
