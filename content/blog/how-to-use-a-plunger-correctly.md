Almost everyone owns a plunger, and almost everyone uses it wrong. Not because plunging is complicated, but because most people grab whichever plunger is under the sink, jam it over the drain, and pump as fast and hard as they can. That approach sometimes works by accident, but more often it just splashes water everywhere and leaves the clog exactly where it was.

There's an actual technique here, and once you know it, a plunger becomes a genuinely reliable tool instead of a frustrating one.

## Mistake 1: Using the wrong type of plunger

There are two main types, and they're built for different jobs.

- **Cup plunger** (the classic dome-shaped rubber cup on a stick): designed for flat surfaces like sinks and tubs, where the drain opening is flush with the surface.
- **Flange plunger** (a cup with an extra folded rubber flap extending from the bottom): designed specifically for toilets, since that flap extends down into the curved toilet drain to form a proper seal.

Using a flat cup plunger on a toilet, or a flange plunger on a flat sink drain, makes it much harder to get an airtight seal, which is the entire foundation of how plunging works.

## Mistake 2: Not creating a real seal

A plunger doesn't clear a clog through brute force. It works by creating pressure changes, pushing water and air forward on the down-stroke and pulling it back on the up-stroke, which loosens whatever is blocking the pipe. None of that works if air is leaking in around the edges of the plunger instead of staying trapped inside the seal.

For sinks and tubs, add enough water to fully submerge the plunger cup before you start, since a dry seal doesn't transfer pressure nearly as well. Press the plunger down firmly to expel the air first, then make sure the entire rim is making solid contact with the surface around the drain.

## Mistake 3: Plunging too fast

The instinct is to pump hard and fast, like you're trying to punch through the clog. That's backwards. Fast, aggressive plunging tends to just break the seal repeatedly instead of building useful pressure. The right motion is a slow, steady push down, then a sharper pull up, since the upward pull is often what actually dislodges the blockage by creating suction.

## The correct technique, step by step

1. Choose the right plunger type for the fixture (cup for sinks and tubs, flange for toilets).
2. If there's an overflow opening nearby (like in a bathroom sink or tub), plug it with a wet rag so you don't lose pressure through it.
3. Make sure there's enough water in the bowl or basin to fully cover the plunger cup.
4. Position the plunger straight over the drain and press down slowly to push out trapped air and form a seal.
5. Plunge with 10 to 15 steady strokes, pushing down firmly and pulling up sharply, keeping the seal intact the whole time.
6. Pull the plunger away quickly on the last stroke and check if water drains normally.
7. Repeat if needed, but if three or four rounds of proper plunging don't clear it, the clog is likely beyond what plunging alone can fix.

## When plunging isn't enough

If you're using the right plunger, forming a good seal, and using the correct technique but the drain is still slow or blocked, the clog is probably further down the pipe than a plunger's pressure can reach, or it's something denser than plunging is designed to move.
