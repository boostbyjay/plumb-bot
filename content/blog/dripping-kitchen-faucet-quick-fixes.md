There's a specific kind of tired that comes from lying in bed listening to a faucet drip two rooms away. It's not loud, it's not dangerous, and yet it somehow manages to be one of the most irritating sounds in a house. Beyond the annoyance, that drip adds up fast. A faucet leaking at just one drop per second can waste over 3,000 gallons a year, which shows up on your water bill whether you notice it or not.

Before you assume your faucet needs to be replaced, run through a few quick checks. Kitchen faucets get used constantly, and a lot of drips come from something simple rather than a major internal failure.

## Check 1: Is the handle actually loose?

Grab the handle and gently wiggle it side to side and up and down. If there's noticeable play, the internal mounting screw may have backed out over time from regular use. Most single-handle kitchen faucets have a small setscrew, often accessed with an Allen wrench, hidden under the handle or a decorative cap. Tightening that screw can stop a drip caused by a handle that isn't seating fully against the valve underneath.

## Check 2: Clean out the aerator

The aerator is the small mesh screen screwed onto the tip of the spout, and it's an easy thing to overlook when you're troubleshooting a leak. Mineral deposits and debris can build up inside it, which sometimes causes water to back up and drip from unexpected spots, especially right after you shut the water off. Unscrew it by hand or with pliers wrapped in a cloth to avoid scratching the finish, then soak it in white vinegar for 15 to 20 minutes to dissolve buildup before rinsing and screwing it back in.

## Check 3: Look at the cartridge

If tightening the handle and cleaning the aerator don't help, the cartridge is next on the list. Most modern kitchen faucets use a cartridge rather than the old rubber washer setup, and cartridges wear out with age just like anything else with moving parts. Signs the cartridge is the problem include:

- Dripping that continues no matter how tightly you turn the handle off
- Water temperature that's hard to control or drifts on its own
- A handle that feels loose or "mushy" even after tightening the setscrew

Replacing a cartridge means shutting off the water, removing the handle, pulling out the old cartridge, and pressing in a new one that matches your faucet's brand and model. It's a more involved job than the first two checks, but still doable for a lot of homeowners with basic tools.

## Tools that make this easier

- Allen wrench set (often included with the faucet originally)
- Adjustable pliers or a wrench
- White vinegar for soaking
- A small cup or bowl to hold tiny screws
- A flashlight, since under-sink lighting is rarely good

## When persistent drips mean something bigger

If you've tightened the handle, cleaned the aerator, and even swapped the cartridge and the faucet still won't stop dripping, something else is going on. It could be a corroded valve seat, a supply line connection that's starting to fail, or mineral damage inside the faucet body itself that a new cartridge can't fix. Water stains on the cabinet floor below the sink, or a faucet base that feels loose no matter what you tighten, are both signs it's time to stop troubleshooting on your own.
