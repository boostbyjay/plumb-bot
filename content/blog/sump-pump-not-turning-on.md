Water is rising in the pit and your sump pump isn't doing anything about it. This is one of those situations where a few quick checks can make the difference between a non-event and a flooded basement, but it's also a situation where you need to be careful about the fact that water and electricity are involved.

## A Quick Safety Note Before You Start

If there's already standing water on the basement floor near any electrical outlets, cords, or the pump's power connection, don't wade in to troubleshoot. Assess visually from dry ground first, and if you're unsure whether it's safe, treat it as unsafe.

## Check 1: Power and the Outlet

Sump pumps typically plug into a dedicated outlet, often a GFCI (ground-fault circuit interrupter) outlet given the water exposure. GFCI outlets are designed to trip and cut power at the first sign of a ground fault, which is a safety feature, but it also means a tripped GFCI is a common, simple reason the pump has no power at all.

1. Locate the outlet the pump is plugged into
2. Look for a small reset button on the outlet itself, and press it if it's tripped
3. Confirm the plug is fully seated in the outlet
4. Check your breaker panel for a tripped breaker feeding that circuit

## Check 2: The Float Switch

The float switch is what tells the pump when to turn on based on water level, and it's a common point of failure:

- **Stuck against the pit wall**: if the pit is narrow or the pump isn't centered well, the float can get physically wedged against the side and never rise enough to trigger the pump
- **Tangled in the power cord**: the float needs room to move freely, and a cord that's coiled too close can restrict its movement
- **Debris in the pit**: dirt, gravel, or sediment settling in the pit can interfere with float movement over time

Gently check that the float has full range of motion and isn't obstructed by anything.

## Check 3: A Jammed Impeller

If the pump has power and the float is moving freely, but the motor still won't engage or sounds like it's straining, the impeller (the internal rotating component that actually moves water) may be jammed with debris, sediment, or small objects that ended up in the pit. This is a more involved check, since it may mean pulling the pump to inspect it directly, and it's an area where getting hands near electrical components in a wet pit deserves real caution.

## A Quick Troubleshooting Order

1. Check the outlet, GFCI reset button, and breaker
2. Confirm the float switch moves freely and isn't obstructed
3. Manually lift the float by hand to see if the pump engages at all (a useful diagnostic if it's safe to do)
4. If it engages manually but not automatically, the float switch itself may be failing
5. If it doesn't engage even manually, the issue is likely electrical or the motor itself

## When This Is Bigger Than a Quick Fix

If you've checked the outlet, the breaker, and the float switch, and the pump still won't run, especially during active heavy rain, this isn't the time to keep experimenting. A failed sump pump during a storm is a genuine emergency given how quickly water can accumulate. ProFlow Plumbing can respond quickly to get water moving again, or in the meantime, a portable backup pump can buy you time while a permanent fix gets sorted out. Either way, don't wait until the basement is already wet to make the call.
