Watch water swirl slowly around a kitchen sink instead of disappearing, and there's a good chance you already know the culprit, even if you haven't wanted to think about it: grease. Kitchen sinks handle a very different kind of waste than bathroom drains, and it shows up in exactly where the clogs tend to form.

## Why kitchen sinks clog differently than other drains

Every time you rinse a greasy pan or pour off cooking oil, that fat goes down the drain as a warm liquid and travels only a short distance before your pipes cool it back down. Grease solidifies as it cools, coating the inside of the pipe and gradually narrowing the passage, especially at the P-trap, the curved section of pipe under the sink. Food particles, coffee grounds, and soap scum stick to that greasy coating and build the clog up further over time.

## Step 1: Check the P-trap first

The P-trap is usually the first and most accessible place a kitchen clog forms, and it's a manageable job to inspect yourself.

**What you'll need:**

- Bucket
- Adjustable wrench or channel-lock pliers
- Old towel
- Rubber gloves

**Steps:**

1. Place the bucket directly under the P-trap to catch water and debris.
2. Loosen the slip nuts on either end of the trap by hand or with pliers.
3. Slide the trap down and empty its contents into the bucket. You'll likely find a solid mass of grease, food debris, or both.
4. Clean the trap out thoroughly, including scraping the inside walls, then rinse it before reinstalling.
5. Reattach the trap, hand-tighten the slip nuts, then run water to check for leaks and confirm the drain flows freely.

## Step 2: If the trap was clean, or the clog persists

If the trap itself wasn't the problem, or draining is still slow after cleaning it, the blockage is likely further down the line, either in the branch drain leading to your main stack or, if multiple fixtures are affected, somewhere deeper in the system. Try a hand-crank drum auger fed through the drain opening, working it gently until you feel resistance, then breaking through and pulling back slowly. This can clear a clog that's just past the trap without needing to open any pipes.

## When it's more than a kitchen clog

A few signs suggest the problem isn't isolated to your kitchen sink's own drain line:

- Other fixtures in the house are also draining slowly or gurgling
- The clog comes back within a few weeks of clearing it
- You notice a sewage-like smell near the kitchen drain
- Water backs up into the sink from the dishwasher or disposal

Any of these point toward a blockage further down the main line rather than a simple grease buildup at the trap, and a hand auger typically won't reach far enough or have the power to clear it.

## Prevention that actually works

The best fix for a grease-clogged kitchen drain is not creating one in the first place. Wipe greasy pans with a paper towel before washing them, pour cooking oil into a container for disposal rather than down the sink, and run hot water for thirty seconds after washing dishes to help flush any residue through before it has a chance to cool and stick.

If you've cleaned the trap, tried a hand auger, and you're still dealing with slow drainage, or if the clog keeps returning no matter what you do, it's a good sign the blockage has moved beyond what a homeowner's tools can reach.
