You turn the handle expecting a normal flow and get a weak trickle instead. It's a small thing, but it's the kind of small thing that makes brushing your teeth or washing your hands take twice as long as it should. The good news is that low pressure at a single bathroom faucet is one of the more diagnosable plumbing problems out there, and it usually doesn't require any special tools to figure out.

Work through the possibilities in this order, starting with the simplest.

## Start with the aerator

The aerator is the small screw-on screen at the tip of the faucet spout, and it's the number one cause of reduced flow at a single fixture. Mineral deposits from hard water build up inside the tiny mesh over months of use, gradually choking off flow until one day you notice it's really slowed down. This is worth checking first because it takes five minutes and costs nothing.

**Quick aerator test:** Unscrew the aerator by hand (use pliers wrapped in a cloth if it's stuck), then turn the faucet on without it. If the flow is strong and normal with the aerator removed, you've found your culprit. Soak the aerator in white vinegar for 20 to 30 minutes to dissolve the buildup, scrub gently with an old toothbrush, rinse, and reinstall.

## Check the cartridge or valve next

If removing the aerator doesn't restore full pressure, the problem is likely inside the faucet itself. Mineral buildup doesn't stop at the aerator. It can also coat the cartridge or valve mechanism, restricting flow even when everything else is clear. This is a bit more involved to check since it means shutting off the water and partially disassembling the faucet, but if you already identified your faucet type and are comfortable with basic repairs, cleaning or replacing the cartridge often solves it.

## Check the shut-off valve under the sink

It sounds almost too simple, but a partially closed shut-off valve under the sink is a common and completely overlooked cause of low pressure. These valves get bumped, or someone (maybe a past owner, maybe you) closed them partway during a previous repair and never fully reopened them. Look under the sink, find the valve or valves feeding your faucet, and make sure they're turned fully counterclockwise to the open position.

## Rule out a whole-house issue

If low pressure isn't limited to this one faucet, the cause is somewhere upstream of it. Signs you're dealing with a broader issue:

- Multiple fixtures throughout the house have weak flow, not just this one
- The problem appeared suddenly across the whole home rather than gradually at one sink
- Your water pressure regulator (if your home has one) is old or was never adjusted
- Neighbors have mentioned similar issues, suggesting a municipal supply problem

A whole-house pressure problem points toward something like a failing pressure regulator, sediment building up in the main line, or in older homes, corroded galvanized pipes narrowing from the inside. None of these are aerator-level fixes.

## When to call in a professional

If you've cleaned the aerator, checked the shut-off valve, and confirmed the issue is isolated to one faucet but pressure still hasn't improved, there may be a deeper blockage or a failing part inside the faucet body that needs replacement rather than cleaning. And if the low pressure turns out to be a whole-house issue, that's squarely outside what a homeowner should try to diagnose alone, since it can involve your main water line or the pressure regulator itself.
