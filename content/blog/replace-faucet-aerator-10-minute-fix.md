Most plumbing repairs come with some risk of making things worse. Strip a fitting, over-tighten a nut, or forget to shut off the water first, and a small job can turn into a bigger one fast. Replacing a faucet aerator isn't one of those jobs. It's about as close to foolproof as plumbing gets, no water shutoff required, no risk of flooding, and it takes less time than waiting for your coffee to brew.

If your faucet has been spraying unevenly, running weak, or has a visible ring of white or greenish crust around the tip, this is very likely your fix.

## What an aerator actually does

The aerator is the small cylindrical screen threaded onto the very end of your faucet spout. It mixes air into the water stream, which reduces splashing, controls flow rate, and saves water compared to an unrestricted stream. Over time it collects mineral deposits, debris, and occasionally small bits of sediment from the pipes, which is why flow gets weaker or the spray pattern turns lopsided.

## What you'll need

- A new aerator (bring the old one to match the size and thread type, or check if it's male or female threaded)
- Channel-lock pliers or an adjustable wrench (only needed if it's stuck)
- A soft cloth to protect the finish
- Plumber's tape (optional, for a cleaner seal)

## Step 1: Remove the old aerator

Wrap the cloth around the aerator to protect the faucet's finish, then turn it counterclockwise by hand. Most aerators loosen easily this way. If it won't budge, wrap the cloth around it again and use pliers for extra grip, turning gently so you don't damage the faucet spout threads.

## Step 2: Take a look at what you're dealing with

Once it's off, you'll usually see exactly why flow was weak: a crusty buildup of mineral deposits clogging the mesh screen. If the aerator itself looks fine and you just want to upgrade it, or it's cracked or corroded, that's your cue to replace rather than clean it.

## Step 3: Clean the threads

Before installing the new one, wipe down the exposed threads on the faucet spout with the cloth to remove any grit or old plumber's tape residue. A clean thread means a smooth, leak-free connection.

## Step 4: Screw in the new aerator

Thread the new aerator on by hand, turning clockwise, until it's snug. Give it a final quarter-turn with the pliers (cloth still wrapped around it) if needed, but avoid overtightening since aerators are usually made of relatively soft metal or plastic.

## Step 5: Test it

Turn the faucet on and check for leaks around the base of the aerator, and confirm the spray pattern looks even and full. If it's dripping from the connection point, snug it another quarter-turn.

## Why this is worth doing even if flow seems fine

Aerators are cheap, usually just a few dollars, and swapping them every year or two as routine maintenance prevents the gradual pressure loss most people just get used to and stop noticing. It's also worth checking anytime you notice water tasting or smelling slightly off, since sediment trapped in an old aerator can sometimes be the source.

If you've replaced the aerator and pressure is still weak, or water is now leaking from somewhere other than the aerator connection, that points to a problem further inside the faucet or plumbing system, not something a new aerator will solve.
