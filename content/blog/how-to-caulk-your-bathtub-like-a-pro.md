Caulk around a bathtub does one important job: it keeps water from sneaking behind the tub and into the wall or subfloor every time you shower. When it cracks, peels, or grows mildew, it's not just a cosmetic problem. It's a gap that lets water go exactly where you don't want it, sometimes for months before you notice any actual damage. Redoing it properly is a manageable weekend project if you take it step by step.

## Tools and materials

- Caulk removal tool or a stiff putty knife
- Utility knife
- Rubbing alcohol and clean rags
- Painter's tape
- 100 percent silicone caulk (specifically labeled for kitchen and bath use)
- Caulking gun
- A caulk smoothing tool, or a gloved finger dipped in soapy water

## Step 1: Remove the old caulk completely

Run a utility knife along both edges of the existing caulk line to loosen it, then use a caulk removal tool or putty knife to scrape it out. Get as much of the old caulk out as you can, including any bits that have worked their way into the corners. Leftover old caulk under a new bead prevents a proper seal and often causes the new caulk to fail early.

## Step 2: Clean and dry the surface thoroughly

This step gets skipped more than any other, and it's the reason a lot of DIY caulk jobs fail within a few months. Wipe the entire area down with rubbing alcohol to remove any soap scum, residue, or mildew, and let it dry completely. Silicone caulk needs a clean, dry, oil-free surface to bond properly. If there's any mildew staining that alcohol doesn't fully remove, a diluted bleach solution can help, just make sure the surface is completely dry before moving on.

## Step 3: Tape off clean lines

Run painter's tape along both sides of the gap you're about to caulk, leaving just the gap itself exposed. This isn't strictly necessary, but it makes a huge difference in how clean and professional the final line looks, especially for anyone caulking for the first time. It also protects the tile and tub surface from stray caulk.

## Step 4: Apply and tool the bead

Load your caulking gun and cut the tip of the caulk tube at a slight angle, with an opening roughly matching the width of the gap you're filling. Apply steady, even pressure as you move the gun in one continuous motion along the seam. Resist the urge to stop and start repeatedly, since that's what creates uneven blobs and gaps.

Immediately after applying the caulk, run a caulk smoothing tool or a finger (dipped in a bit of soapy water to prevent sticking) along the bead in one smooth motion. This presses the caulk into the gap for a better seal and gives you that clean, slightly concave finished look.

## Step 5: Remove the tape and let it cure

Pull the painter's tape off immediately after tooling the caulk, while it's still wet, rather than waiting for it to dry. Removing tape from dried caulk tends to pull up parts of the bead along with it, undoing your clean line.

Most silicone caulk needs 24 hours to fully cure, though it may feel dry to the touch much sooner. Avoid using the shower or tub during that window so water doesn't disrupt the seal before it's fully set.

## Why this matters more than it looks like it should

Bad or missing caulk lets water seep behind the tub surround and into the wall cavity or subfloor, where it can cause hidden rot, mold, and eventually a much more expensive repair than a tube of caulk and an afternoon.
