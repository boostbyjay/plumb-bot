You turn off the washing machine or the dishwasher finishes filling, and somewhere behind the wall you hear a loud, single bang, sometimes loud enough to make you jump. It's not a ghost and it's not your pipes settling. It's a phenomenon plumbers call water hammer, and while it's usually not an emergency, it's worth understanding what's actually happening.

## The Simple Physics Behind the Bang

Water moving through your pipes has momentum, the same way a car has momentum when it's driving down the road. When a valve, like the one inside a washing machine or a quick-closing dishwasher solenoid, snaps shut suddenly, that moving water has nowhere to go. The energy has to go somewhere, so it turns into a shockwave that travels back through the pipe and slams into the nearest bend or fitting, which is what you hear as a bang.

This is different from the gradual whoosh of a sink faucet, which you close slowly with your hand. Water hammer almost always comes from appliances with automatic valves: washing machines, dishwashers, ice makers, and some modern toilet fill valves.

## Why It's More Than Just Annoying

An occasional bang isn't going to hurt anything. But if it happens every time an appliance cycles, day after day, that repeated shockwave puts real stress on your plumbing:

- Loosens pipe fittings and joints over time
- Can wear out valve components faster than normal
- Stresses solder joints on copper pipe
- May eventually contribute to a slow leak at a weakened connection

Think of it like slamming a car door every day for ten years versus closing it gently. Eventually something gives.

## What's Usually Causing It

A few common culprits show up again and again:

- **Missing or waterlogged air chambers**: older homes were built with short vertical sections of pipe (air chambers) near fast-closing valves, designed to cushion the shock with a pocket of trapped air. Over time these can fill with water and stop working.
- **High water pressure**: pressure above the recommended range makes any shockwave more forceful. If you haven't checked yours, it's worth testing.
- **Loose pipes**: pipes that aren't properly strapped or secured to framing can vibrate and bang against studs or joists even without true water hammer.
- **Missing water hammer arrestors on newer fixtures**: modern code often requires these near quick-closing valves, and older installations may simply not have them.

## Simple Fixes You Can Try

If the noise is new or occasional, start with the easy stuff. Draining your plumbing system can sometimes recharge waterlogged air chambers: shut off the main water valve, open the highest faucet in the house and the lowest one to drain the lines completely, then turn the main valve back on. This lets air refill those chambers.

If that doesn't help, or your home never had air chambers to begin with, a mechanical water hammer arrestor, a small canister with a cushioned air pocket, can be installed at the problem fixture. These are relatively inexpensive parts, but installing one usually means accessing the pipe near the appliance, which sometimes requires opening a wall.

If the banging is loud, frequent, or coming from multiple fixtures, or if you've tried draining the system without success, it's a good sign the fix needs more than a DIY air recharge.
