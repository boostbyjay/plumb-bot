If you've read through much of this blog, you've probably noticed a pattern by now. Some plumbing problems really are simple enough to handle with a wrench, a Saturday afternoon, and a trip to the hardware store. Others look simple on the surface but hide enough complexity, or enough risk, that they're better left to someone who does this for a living. After covering everything from dripping faucets to sewer lines, it seems worth pulling all of that into one clear, practical list.

## Safe to DIY

These are jobs where the tools are inexpensive, the risk of making things worse is low, and a mistake is usually easy to correct.

- Replacing a faucet aerator or showerhead
- Plunging a clogged toilet or slow sink drain with the right technique and the right plunger
- Cleaning a P-trap under a kitchen or bathroom sink
- Swapping a worn toilet flapper or adjusting the fill valve on a running toilet
- Clearing hair from a shower or bathtub drain
- Testing your water pressure with an inexpensive gauge
- Flushing sediment from a water heater on a regular schedule
- Winterizing outdoor faucets and disconnecting garden hoses before the first freeze
- Testing a sump pump by pouring water into the pit before storm season
- Cleaning gutters and adding downspout extensions to protect your foundation

None of these require specialized training, and getting them slightly wrong the first time rarely leads to serious damage. They're also exactly the kind of routine maintenance that prevents bigger problems down the line, whether that's a clogged drain, a failing water heater, or a wet basement.

## DIY with caution

These jobs are still within reach for a patient, careful homeowner, but they come with a real chance of turning into something bigger if a step gets skipped or a fitting doesn't cooperate.

- Replacing a faucet or fixing a leaky valve, where corroded shutoff valves and stripped supply line fittings can quietly turn a simple swap into a flooded cabinet
- Installing a new kitchen or bathroom faucet, especially in a cramped under-sink workspace
- Snaking a drain with a hand-crank auger, which works for surface clogs but shouldn't be pushed past resistance you don't understand
- Thawing a frozen pipe, where careless use of heat or missing a hidden burst can make a bad situation worse
- Adjusting a water heater's temperature or troubleshooting a pilot light, both of which involve gas or electrical components deserving real respect
- Regrouting tile or caulking a tub, which is fine cosmetically but can mask a deeper leak if you don't check for soft walls or loose tile first

The common thread here is that these jobs are approachable if you follow the steps carefully and stop the moment something doesn't match what you expected. Rushing, forcing a stuck fitting, or skipping the shutoff step is usually where these go wrong.

## Always call a professional

Some plumbing problems fall outside what any homeowner should attempt, either because of the tools and training required, or because getting it wrong risks your home, your health, or both.

- Sewer line problems, including tree root intrusion, repeated backups, or multiple drains clogging at once
- Slab leaks or any leak hidden behind a wall or under a foundation
- Gas line and gas water heater repairs, or any pilot light issue that keeps recurring
- Water heater replacement, especially switching between gas and electric
- Septic tank pumping and system repairs
- Sump pump replacement or backup system installation
- Main water line repairs or replacing a home's primary shutoff valve
- Any active flooding or emergency where you can't quickly identify and stop the source

These aren't jobs where confidence and a YouTube video are enough substitutes for licensing, the right equipment, and hands-on experience. The cost of a mistake, whether that's a gas leak, sewage backing into your home, or water damage that spreads through multiple rooms, is simply too high to treat as a learning opportunity.

## The real skill worth building

More than any single repair, the most valuable plumbing skill a homeowner can develop is recognizing which category a problem falls into before diving in. A running toilet and a running sewer line sound similar in casual conversation, but one is a five-minute fix and the other can flood your basement. Learning to tell them apart, and knowing your home's main shutoff valve before you ever need it in an emergency, will save you more money and stress than any single repair guide.
