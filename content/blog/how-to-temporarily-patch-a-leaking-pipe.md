Water is spraying, dripping, or pooling from a pipe, and it's late enough that a plumber can't be there in the next ten minutes. Here's exactly what to do to stabilize the situation without making it worse.

## Step 1: Shut off the water first

Before touching the pipe itself, stop the flow of water. If the leak is isolated to one fixture, like a sink or toilet, there's often a local shutoff valve nearby you can close. For anything else, or if you can't find a local shutoff, go straight to your home's main water shutoff valve and turn it fully off.

This step matters more than the patch itself. Attempting to wrap or clamp a pipe that's still under full pressure is far less effective and can even make the leak worse, since pressurized water will find and exploit any gap in your temporary fix.

## Step 2: Drain the remaining water

With the supply shut off, open a faucet somewhere lower in the house (or the affected fixture itself) to relieve any remaining pressure in the line and let residual water drain out. This gives you a much drier, calmer pipe to work with.

## Step 3: Dry the pipe thoroughly

Use a towel to dry the area around the leak completely. Nearly every temporary patching method, whether tape, clamps, or epoxy, bonds far better to a dry surface than a wet one. Skipping this step is one of the most common reasons a temporary patch fails almost immediately.

## Step 4: Apply your patch

- **Self-fusing silicone tape:** stretch it tightly as you wrap, overlapping each layer, and extend the wrap several inches beyond the leak point in both directions for a stronger seal.
- **A pipe repair clamp:** position the rubber gasket directly over the leak, then tighten the clamp evenly around it, checking that it's centered before fully tightening.
- **Epoxy putty:** knead the two parts together until the color is uniform, then press and mold it firmly over the leak, holding pressure for the time specified on the packaging while it begins to set.

## Step 5: Turn the water back on slowly, and watch closely

Once your patch is in place, turn the main or local shutoff back on slowly rather than all at once, and keep an eye on the patched area for the next several minutes. Some drip or seepage at the edges is not unusual, but if the patch fails outright, turn the water back off immediately.

## This is a stopgap, not a fix

It's worth repeating: none of these methods repair the actual cause of the leak, whether that's a corroded section of pipe, a failed joint, or freeze damage that cracked the pipe wall. A temporary patch on a pressurized system can fail without warning, sometimes hours or days later, especially if water pressure fluctuates or the pipe shifts slightly.

Once you've stabilized things and everyone in the house is safe, it's best to leave the water main off (or the affected fixture's line off) until ProFlow Plumbing can properly diagnose and repair the pipe. Treating the patch as a real fix, rather than a bridge to a professional repair, is how a manageable leak turns into a much bigger and more expensive problem.
