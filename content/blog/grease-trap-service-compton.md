## California grease trap regulations

Under California's Uniform Fire Code and health code (CalCode), food service establishments must install and maintain a grease trap or grease interceptor sized appropriately for their kitchen's output. The maintenance requirements include:

- Pumping the trap before the FOG (fats, oils, and grease) layer plus settled solids exceeds 25% of the trap's capacity
- Keeping detailed records of each pumping — dates, volumes, and the hauler's information
- Making these records available during health inspections
- Installing the correct trap size for your kitchen's flow rate — an undersized trap will fail regardless of how often you pump it

## How often should you pump?

That depends on your kitchen. A high-volume restaurant frying food all day will fill a trap faster than a coffee shop or deli. Most Compton restaurants find that quarterly service keeps them compliant, but larger operations with deep fryers running continuously may need monthly service. The only way to know for sure is to have your trap inspected regularly and track the buildup rate.

## Warning signs your trap needs attention

- Slow-draining floor drains in the kitchen and prep area
- Greasy film on top of standing water in floor drains
- Foul, sour odors coming from drains
- Kitchen sink draining slowly despite normal use
- Health inspector comments about FOG buildup

## The real cost of a grease trap emergency

A grease trap failure during business hours can force your kitchen to shut down. Emergency pumping costs significantly more than scheduled service. Health code violations can result in fines and temporary closure. And once word gets out that a restaurant had a plumbing shutdown, the reputational damage can last longer than the cleanup.
