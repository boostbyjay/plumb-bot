When it's time to replace a water heater, most homeowners default to whatever fuel type they already have. That's often the right call, but it's worth understanding the actual differences before you commit, especially if you're building new, renovating, or your current setup has never quite kept up with your household's needs.

## Upfront cost

Electric tank water heaters are generally less expensive to purchase and install than gas models, mainly because they don't require venting for exhaust gases. Gas units cost more upfront but often make up some of that difference over time through lower operating costs, depending on local utility rates.

## Operating cost

This is where gas usually pulls ahead. Natural gas is typically cheaper per unit of energy than electricity in most parts of the country, so a gas water heater often costs less to run month to month, even though it costs more to install. Electric rates and gas rates vary a lot by region, so this isn't universal, but it's the general pattern in most markets.

## Recovery speed

Recovery speed refers to how quickly a tank reheats water after it's been used up, which matters most for larger households or back-to-back showers. Gas burners heat water faster than standard electric heating elements, so a gas tank of a given size typically keeps up with heavier demand better than an electric tank of the same size.

## Installation complexity

This is the trade-off that catches people off guard. Gas water heaters need a proper venting system to safely exhaust combustion gases, along with a gas line sized correctly for the unit. Electric water heaters just need an adequate electrical circuit, which is usually simpler in an existing home that already has one in place. Converting from electric to gas, or vice versa, is a bigger project than swapping in a new tank of the same fuel type, since it usually means adding or removing venting and running new gas or electrical lines.

## A quick side-by-side

- **Electric:** lower upfront cost, simpler installation, generally higher operating cost, slower recovery
- **Gas:** higher upfront cost, requires proper venting, generally lower operating cost, faster recovery
- **Switching fuel types:** always more involved than a like-for-like replacement, and it touches your home's gas line or electrical panel

## Reliability during power outages

One factor homeowners sometimes overlook is what happens during a power outage. A standard gas water heater with a standing pilot light will often keep producing hot water even when the electricity is out, since it doesn't rely on power to ignite or run. Gas units with electronic ignition, and all electric units, will stop working until power is restored. If you live somewhere prone to outages, this can be a meaningful point in gas's favor beyond just operating cost.

## Why switching fuel types isn't a weekend project

If you're currently on electric and considering gas (or the reverse), the water heater itself is often the easy part. The real work is in the supporting infrastructure: running a new gas line and venting through the roof or wall for a gas conversion, or upgrading your electrical panel and circuit for an electric conversion. Both of these involve code requirements and safety considerations that go well beyond installing the tank.
