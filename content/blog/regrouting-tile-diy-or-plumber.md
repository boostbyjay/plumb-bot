Grout doesn't last forever, and most homeowners will regrout a shower or tub surround at some point. Some of the time it's a straightforward weekend project. Other times, cracked and crumbling grout is the visible symptom of a plumbing problem that regrouting alone won't fix, no matter how carefully you do it.

## When regrouting is a genuine DIY project

If the grout itself is simply old, discolored, or has small surface cracks but the tile behind it is solid and the wall or floor shows no other signs of trouble, this is a manageable project for most homeowners. Signs you're in this category:

- Grout is cracked or crumbling only in a thin surface layer
- Tiles are firmly attached with no movement when pressed
- No soft spots, discoloration, or odor in the surrounding wall or floor
- The issue is limited to appearance, not function

**Tools and materials for a basic regrout:**

- Grout saw or oscillating multi-tool with a grout removal blade
- Grout float
- New grout (sanded or unsanded, matched to your existing joint width)
- Grout sealer
- Sponge and bucket

The process is mostly patience: remove the old grout to a consistent depth, vacuum out the dust, mix and apply new grout with the float held at an angle, wipe away the excess with a damp sponge before it fully cures, and seal it once dry. It's slow, repetitive work, but it doesn't require specialized plumbing knowledge.

## When it's a sign of something bigger

Grout failure isn't always just age. Sometimes it's a symptom of water getting behind the tile from a leak, and no amount of fresh grout on the surface will fix that. Watch for these red flags before you commit to a regrout:

- **Tiles that flex, shift, or sound hollow when tapped.** This usually means the substrate underneath has been compromised by moisture and the tile is no longer bonded properly.
- **Soft or spongy drywall near the tile.** If you press on the wall around the shower and it gives even slightly, water has likely been soaking into the wall material.
- **A musty smell that persists after cleaning.** This often points to trapped moisture behind the wall rather than surface mildew.
- **Grout failure concentrated at a specific seam, like the corner or the base of the shower.** Localized failure, rather than general wear across the whole surface, often points to a specific leak point like a failed shower pan seal.
- **Water stains on the ceiling below, if the bathroom is on an upper floor.**

If any of these apply, the grout is telling you about a problem that started underneath it. Regrouting over it will look fine for a while and then fail again in the same spot, because the actual source of moisture was never addressed.

## Why this distinction matters

Retiling and regrouting after a hidden leak has taken hold is a much bigger job than a simple grout refresh, since it typically means removing tile, drying and possibly replacing the wall material, and addressing the leak itself before anything goes back up. Doing the cosmetic work first just delays and hides the real problem.

If your grout situation matches the simple, surface-level list, go ahead and tackle it yourself.
