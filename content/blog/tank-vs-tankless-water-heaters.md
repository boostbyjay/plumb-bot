Ask five homeowners about tankless water heaters and you'll probably get five different opinions, usually strong ones. The truth is more nuanced than "tankless is always better" or "tank heaters are outdated." Here's an honest look at how they compare.

## Upfront cost and lifespan

Traditional tank water heaters cost less to purchase and, in most cases, less to install, especially if you're replacing an existing tank with another one in the same spot. Tankless units cost more for the unit itself, and installation is often more involved, particularly if your home's gas line or electrical service needs upgrading to support the higher demand a tankless unit places on it during operation.

Lifespan is one of tankless's clearest advantages, though. A well-maintained tank water heater typically lasts 8 to 12 years. Tankless units often last 15 to 20 years, roughly double, which helps offset some of the higher upfront cost over time.

## Space savings

Tankless water heaters are wall-mounted and much smaller than a standard 40 to 50 gallon tank, freeing up real floor space in a utility closet, garage, or basement. If space is tight or you're trying to reclaim a closet for storage, this is a meaningful practical benefit.

## Hot water supply: endless vs. simultaneous use

Tankless units heat water on demand as it flows through, which means you genuinely won't run out of hot water mid-shower the way you can with a tank that's been depleted. The trade-off is simultaneous use. Every tankless unit has a maximum flow rate it can heat at once, and running a shower, the dishwasher, and a washing machine at the same time can exceed that capacity, resulting in lukewarm water across all three. Tank heaters, by contrast, store a fixed volume of hot water that multiple fixtures can draw from at once, though that volume is finite and can run out under heavy simultaneous demand.

## A quick side-by-side

- **Tank:** lower upfront cost, 8 to 12 year lifespan, larger footprint, hot water can run out but handles simultaneous use up to that stored volume
- **Tankless:** higher upfront cost, 15 to 20 year lifespan, compact footprint, effectively endless hot water but limited by flow rate during heavy simultaneous use

## Maintenance differences worth knowing

Tank water heaters need a periodic flush to clear sediment, along with the occasional anode rod check, but the maintenance is generally simple enough for a comfortable DIYer to handle. Tankless units also benefit from regular descaling, especially in hard water areas, since mineral buildup inside the compact heat exchanger can reduce efficiency and eventually damage the unit if ignored for years. Because that heat exchanger is a more specialized and expensive component than a simple tank, staying on top of tankless maintenance schedules matters more, not less.

If your household rarely runs multiple hot water fixtures at once and values a lower upfront cost, a tank heater is often the more practical choice. If you regularly deal with running out of hot water, have a larger household, or are planning to stay in your home long enough to benefit from a tankless unit's longer lifespan and lower operating costs, tankless starts to make more financial sense despite the higher initial investment.

## Why conversions need a professional

Switching from tank to tankless is rarely a simple swap. Tankless units typically demand a larger gas line diameter to deliver enough fuel for their higher-output burners, along with different venting than a standard tank. Electric tankless units, meanwhile, often require a significant electrical service upgrade, since they draw much more power in short bursts than a standard tank heater. These aren't cosmetic differences. They involve your home's gas and electrical infrastructure and typically require permitted work.
