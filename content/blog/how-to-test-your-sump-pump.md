A sump pump that hasn't run in months is an unknown quantity, and the worst time to find out it's not working is in the middle of a storm with water already rising in the pit. Testing it takes about five minutes and should be part of your routine before storm season hits, not something you think about only after a flood.

## Why Testing Matters

Sump pumps often sit idle for long stretches, especially during dry periods, and mechanical components that don't get exercised regularly are more prone to sticking or failing when finally called on. A quick manual test confirms the whole system, float switch, motor, and discharge line, is actually functional before you need to rely on it.

## What You'll Need

- A bucket (a standard five-gallon bucket works well)
- Access to your sump pit, usually a lid or cover you can lift
- A flashlight
- A few minutes outside to check the discharge line exit point

## Step-by-Step Test

1. **Locate the sump pit** and remove the cover, if it has one.
2. **Check the water level.** If there's already some water sitting in the pit below the trigger point, that's normal.
3. **Slowly pour a bucket of water into the pit.** You want enough volume to raise the water level to where the float switch would naturally trigger, roughly a full five-gallon bucket in most standard basins.
4. **Watch for the float switch to rise and the pump to kick on.** You should hear the motor engage, and see water level begin dropping as it pumps.
5. **Confirm the pump shuts off** once the water level drops back down, which tells you the float switch is resetting properly, not just triggering.
6. **Check outside** at the discharge line's exit point to confirm water is actually flowing out and away from the foundation, not just that the pump sounds like it's running.

## What a Successful Test Looks Like

- The float rises smoothly with the water level, without getting caught on the pit wall or debris
- The pump engages promptly, without excessive delay
- Water visibly discharges outside, well away from your foundation
- The pump shuts off cleanly once water drops below the trigger point

## Red Flags to Watch For

- **The float switch doesn't trigger** the pump even with water clearly above the trigger height, suggesting a stuck or misaligned float
- **The pump runs but no water comes out the discharge line outside**, which can mean a clogged or frozen line, or a failed check valve
- **Unusual noise**: grinding, rattling, or a labored motor sound
- **The pump runs continuously** and never shuts off, which could mean a stuck float or a check valve allowing water to flow back into the pit

## Checking the Discharge Line, Especially Before Winter

The exterior end of the discharge line is easy to forget, but it's a common point of failure. Before storm season and again before winter, walk outside and make sure the discharge exit isn't blocked by leaves, dirt, or ice, and that it's still directing water well away from your foundation rather than right back toward it.

## Make It a Seasonal Habit

Testing twice a year, once before your rainy season and once before winter, is a reasonable cadence for most homes. It costs nothing but a few minutes and a bucket of water, and it's the only real way to know your pump will do its job when it matters.
