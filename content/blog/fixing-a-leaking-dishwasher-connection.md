Open the cabinet under your sink after a dishwasher cycle and find a puddle, and your first instinct is probably to assume the worst. Before you start pricing a new dishwasher, though, it's worth knowing that most dishwasher leaks trace back to one of just three connection points, and at least one of them is genuinely easy to fix.

## Where dishwasher leaks actually come from

**The water supply line.** This is the line bringing fresh water into the dishwasher, usually connected near the hot water shutoff valve under the sink through a T-fitting. A loose compression nut here can weep slowly, especially after a dishwasher has been moved or serviced.

**The drain hose connection.** The drain hose carries wastewater from the dishwasher, often connecting to the garbage disposal or a dedicated drain fitting under the sink. The clamp holding this hose in place can loosen over time, or the hose itself can develop a crack, particularly near where it bends.

**The air gap or high loop.** Many dishwashers use an air gap (the small chrome cylinder on the sink deck) or a high loop in the drain hose to prevent dirty water from siphoning back into the dishwasher. A clogged air gap can cause water to back up and overflow rather than leak in the traditional sense.

## How to check each one

1. **Run the dishwasher and watch, don't guess.** Open the cabinet doors and actually watch the connections during a fill and drain cycle rather than just checking after the fact. This tells you exactly when the leak happens, which narrows down the cause immediately.
2. **Check the supply line first.** With a flashlight, look at the compression fitting where the supply line meets the shutoff valve and where it meets the dishwasher itself. If you see moisture forming during the fill portion of the cycle, try tightening the compression nut a quarter turn with a wrench, being careful not to overtighten.
3. **Inspect the drain hose and its clamp.** Look along the length of the hose for cracks or a clamp that's slipped loose. If the leak happens during the drain portion of the cycle, this is the most likely spot. A replacement hose clamp is an inexpensive fix.
4. **Clean out the air gap.** If you're seeing water pooling around the small cylinder on your sink deck rather than under the cabinet, unscrew the cap and clear out any debris or buildup inside. This is a common and easy fix that's often mistaken for a bigger leak.

## When it's not actually a connection problem

If you've checked all three points and everything looks tight and clear, but water is still appearing, the leak may be coming from inside the dishwasher itself, such as a worn door gasket, a cracked tub, or an internal valve issue. Those are appliance repair problems rather than plumbing connection issues, and they typically call for either an appliance technician or, if the unit is older, weighing repair cost against replacement.
