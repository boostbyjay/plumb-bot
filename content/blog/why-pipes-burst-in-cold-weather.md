Here's a fact that surprises most homeowners the first time they hear it: when a pipe bursts from freezing, the crack usually isn't at the spot where the ice actually formed. Understanding why helps explain both how frozen pipe damage happens and why the usual prevention advice, like leaving a faucet dripping, actually works.

## It's not the ice itself that breaks the pipe

A common misconception is that expanding ice physically cracks the pipe wall right where it forms, like a balloon stretched until it pops at the thinnest point. That's not quite what happens. Water expands by roughly 9 percent as it freezes, and that expansion does create pressure, but the real damage tends to come from pressure building up between the ice blockage and a closed faucet somewhere downstream.

## How the pressure actually builds

Here's the sequence: ice forms somewhere in a pipe, often in an exposed or poorly insulated section. As freezing continues, that ice blockage grows and seals off that section of pipe completely. Meanwhile, water still sitting in the pipe between the ice blockage and the nearest closed faucet has nowhere to go. As more freezing continues, pressure in that trapped section keeps climbing, since it's sealed on both ends: ice on one side, a closed faucet on the other.

Eventually, that trapped, pressurized water finds the weakest point in the pipe, which is often not the frozen section itself, but a joint, a fitting, or a straight section of pipe some distance away. That's where the crack or split appears, and often it's not visible from the ice blockage's location at all.

## Why this matters for how you thaw a pipe

This is exactly why plumbers always recommend opening the faucet before attempting to thaw a frozen pipe. An open faucet gives that trapped, pressurized water somewhere to go as the ice starts to melt, instead of continuing to build pressure against a dead end. It's also why you should never assume a pipe is fine just because you can't see where it froze. The actual failure point could be entirely separate.

## Why a dripping faucet helps prevent freezing in the first place

A slow, steady drip does two useful things. It relieves pressure that would otherwise build in a closed system as temperatures drop, and it keeps water moving through the pipe. Moving water is significantly more resistant to freezing than still water sitting stagnant in a pipe overnight. On the coldest nights of the year, a small trickle from an at-risk faucet is a genuinely effective, low-cost prevention step.

## Where this typically happens

- Pipes running along exterior walls with minimal insulation
- Pipes in unheated crawlspaces, attics, and garages
- Outdoor faucets and the pipe sections feeding them
- Older homes where pipe insulation wasn't a priority when they were built

## Prevention that follows from the physics

1. Insulate exposed pipes so they're less likely to reach freezing temperatures in the first place
2. Seal drafts around crawlspace vents and pipe entry points
3. Let faucets fed by vulnerable pipes drip slightly on extremely cold nights
4. Keep interior temperatures consistent, even overnight or while away
5. Know where your main shutoff valve is in case a burst does happen
